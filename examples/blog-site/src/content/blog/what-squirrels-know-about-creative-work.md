A squirrel does not wait for the perfect acorn. It notices what is available, takes what it can carry, and keeps moving.

Creative work asks for a similar kind of attention. Most useful ideas do not arrive with a label that says *important*. They appear as a line in a conversation, an odd detail in a book, a problem that will not leave you alone, or a half-finished sketch that seems too small to keep.

The habit is not to turn every finding into a project. It is to collect it.

## Keep a small cache

A notebook, a voice memo, a folder of screenshots—any simple place will do. The point is to lower the cost of saving a thought before the day carries it away.

This is not hoarding. A cache is useful because it is selective. Save the things that create a little pull: a question worth returning to, a phrase that changes how you see a problem, a reference you cannot explain yet.

Over time, the collection becomes more than an archive. It becomes a supply of starting points for the days when the work feels empty.

## Return to unfinished things

Squirrels forget some of what they bury. That is part of the image, not a failure of it. A forgotten acorn can still find a future use.

The same is true of abandoned drafts and unmade experiments. A project you could not solve last season may meet the right constraint, collaborator, or piece of evidence later. Revisiting old material is not always nostalgia. Sometimes it is where new work begins.

Set aside a little time to look through what you have saved. Do not demand that every note prove its value. Notice which ones have become more interesting with distance.

## Make room for the ordinary

Creative momentum is often built from small inputs gathered steadily, not from one dramatic breakthrough. Read widely. Notice your own questions. Leave yourself enough margin to follow an unexpected thread.

The squirrel’s lesson is modest: pay attention, carry something home, and trust that not every useful thing needs to be used immediately.

Work grows in the space between collecting and returning. Keep a few good acorns close.
